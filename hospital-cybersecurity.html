Why it matters

In healthcare, cybersecurity is a patient-safety issue.

A cyber incident can delay care, expose sensitive data or make clinical information unreliable — even without a large-scale attack.

AVAILABILITY

Can we continue care?

Clinical staff need access to applications, examinations and results when patients need them.

INTEGRITY

Can we trust the data?

Patient identities, clinical measurements and treatment records must remain correct and traceable.

CONFIDENTIALITY

Who should have access?

Patient information is sensitive and must be processed securely and appropriately.

People · Security awareness

Security starts with everyday work — not just with IT.

Across healthcare, information security and data protection depend on technology, organizational practices and informed staff.

The goal is to protect information, maintain reliable services and safeguard patient privacy.

Core topics for security awareness

Information securityRecognizing and reducing risks in daily work.

Data protectionHandling personal and patient information responsibly.

Security incidentsRecognizing possible incidents and using reporting procedures.

Building a security culture

Learning happens in several different ways.

Orientation

Introduce secure working habits when new employees begin.

Staff training

Teach staff to recognize risks in realistic healthcare situations.

Information sessions

Use regular briefings to reinforce safe practices.

Advanced learning

Add role-specific training for clinicians, IT staff and device maintainers.

Effective training should be accessible, practical and repeated.

The human factor · Phishing

One convincing message can start a security incident.

Phishing awareness can be strengthened with realistic examples and safe practice exercises. Recognizing an unusual request is only the first step; staff also need to know how to respond.

01 / NOTICE

Unexpected urgency

“Act now” requests can pressure employees into skipping normal checks.

02 / VERIFY

Unusual links or requests

Check unexpected requests through a known, trusted channel.

03 / REPORT

Something feels wrong?

Use the organization's approved route to report suspicious messages.

Everyday security habits

Security is built from small decisions.

01

Know your access

Access information only when it is relevant to your authorized work.

02

Protect sessions

Keep credentials private and secure unattended workstations.

03

Verify requests

Be careful with unfamiliar links, attachments and support requests.

04

Speak up early

Report suspicious activity through approved organizational channels.

A checklist is useful only when employees understand why the behavior matters.
Patient privacy · Data protection

For patients, trust is part of healthcare.

Personal-data protection is a core responsibility in healthcare. Everyday decisions about access, sharing and reporting can affect patient privacy.

Personal health information deserves protection — whether it is in a clinical record, an email or a device-associated system.

Three questions for every employee

Do I need this information?Access must match work-related authorization.

Am I sharing it safely?Use approved channels and intended recipients.

What if something goes wrong?Know how to report a suspected privacy incident.

Security incidents · General response

Recognition matters — reporting is the next step.

A general staff response is to recognize a concern, report it through an approved channel and follow authorized guidance. Exact procedures depend on the organization.

Recognize

Notice unexpected activity or a possible disclosure of information.

Report

Use the official channels defined by the organization.

Cooperate

Provide relevant facts and follow authorized guidance.

Safeguard care

Maintain patient safety while the situation is assessed.

Training effectiveness

Completion is measurable. Awareness is harder.

Training attendance alone does not show whether behavior has improved. Evaluation can combine participation, understanding and response to realistic exercises.

MEASURE 01 · REACH

Who received training?

Completions, participation and coverage over time.

MEASURE 02 · LEARNING

What did people understand?

Knowledge checks and feedback after exercises or training.

MEASURE 03 · RESPONSE

What happens in practice?

Confidence in reporting and response to simulated scenarios.

A useful question: Do employees know what to do when the situation is real?
Medical devices · Cybersecurity

Medical equipment is software — and software has risks.

Connected medical devices can depend on software, networks and vendor maintenance. Security requirements should be considered from procurement through retirement.

IV

Infusion pumps

Drug-delivery settings, device firmware, maintenance software and connectivity.

ECG

Patient monitors

Bedside measurements, alarms, central monitoring and networked data.

VENT

Ventilators

Critical clinical functions, configuration controls and software maintenance.

IT

Shared dependencies

Operating systems, authentication, vendor support and connectivity.

Medical devices · Lifecycle security

Device cybersecurity must be managed throughout its lifecycle.

Assess

Consider cybersecurity requirements and risks before procurement.

Deploy

Configure access, connectivity and responsibilities safely.

Operate

Maintain software, monitor concerns and coordinate vendor support.

Retire

Plan secure replacement, removal of access and handling of stored data.

A connected medical device is part of a larger clinical and technical environment — protecting it also protects continuity of care.
